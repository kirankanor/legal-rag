## Important note

This handbook summarizes selected Indian citizenship judgments and orders for research. A case summary is not the judgment. Always read the original decision before relying on a proposition in litigation or a citizenship application. Case status can change through review, appeal, clarification or later legislation.

## Case selection principle

The cases below are organized around applicant problems rather than merely by court or year. The goal is to answer: what was the factual problem, what statutory provision mattered, what did the court decide, and how should an applicant use the decision without overgeneralizing it?

## Part III - Citizenship by Birth

## Case 3.1 - Rinzin Dolma &amp; connected matters

Court: Delhi High Court Date: 13 April 2017 Citation / Case No.: W.P.(C) 254/2017 and connected petitions Key provisions: Citizenship Act, Section 3; Passport Act

## Facts

The petitioners were born in India at different dates and claimed citizenship under Section 3. The dispute arose when passports were sought and citizenship was questioned.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether persons falling within Section 3 could be treated as Indian citizens for passport purposes.
- 2 How the date of birth and the relevant version of Section 3 affected the claim.

## Decision / holding

The Court held that the petitioners covered by Section 3 were citizens and directed that passports be issued subject to the ordinary checks and formalities under passport law.

## Applicant significance

If your claim is citizenship by birth, the case shows why the exact date of birth and the statutory version applicable to that date are central. It also shows that citizenship status and passport issuance are related but distinct questions.

## Limits of the case

The decision concerned the specific petitioners and their statutory facts. It should not be read as saying every person born in India is a citizen irrespective of date or parental status.

## Part IV - Citizenship by Descent and Foreign Citizenship

## Case 4.1 - Union of India v. Pranav Srinivasan

Court: Supreme Court of India Date: 18 October 2024 Citation / Case No.: 2024 INSC 792; Civil Appeal No. 5932 of 2023 with W.P.(C) No. 123 of 2024 Key provisions: Constitution Articles 8-11; Citizenship Act Sections 5, 8 and 9

## Facts

Pranav Srinivasan was born in Singapore in 1999. His parents, born in India, had acquired Singapore citizenship before his birth. He later sought resumption of Indian citizenship under Section 8(2). The Supreme Court considered whether the statutory route permitted resumption and whether other constitutional/statutory routes automatically applied.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether Section 8(2) permitted resumption on the facts.
- 2 Whether Article 8 or Section 5(1)(b) independently created the claimed entitlement.
- 3 How strictly the statutory eligibility language should be applied.

## Decision / holding

The Supreme Court set aside the High Court decisions and held that the claimant had not resumed Indian citizenship under Section 8(2). It emphasized that citizenship cannot be conferred by departing from the plain statutory requirements. The judgment left open the possibility of applying under Section 5(1)(f), subject to its twelve-month ordinary-residence requirement and the Central Government's statutory relaxation power in special circumstances.

## Applicant significance

For a person born abroad to parents who later acquired foreign citizenship, this is a major warning against relying only on ancestral connection. Identify the exact statutory route and satisfy its conditions.

## Limits of the case

The case is fact-specific. It does not establish a universal rule for every child born abroad; Section 4, the date of birth, parental citizenship at birth, registration requirements and Section 9 issues must be separately examined.

## Case 4.2 - Izhar Ahmad Khan v. Union of India

Court: Supreme Court of India Date: 16 February 1962

Citation / Case No.: 1962 Supreme Court judgment

Key provisions: Constitution Article 9; Citizenship Act Section 9(2); Citizenship Rules evidence provisions

## Facts

The petitioners challenged provisions dealing with determination of foreign citizenship and the statutory consequences of voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether Parliament could provide an evidentiary framework for determining acquisition of foreign citizenship.
- 2 Whether Section 9's statutory scheme was constitutionally valid.

## Decision / holding

The Supreme Court upheld the relevant framework. Section 9 provides that voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship results in cessation of Indian citizenship, while Section 9(2) provides for determination of questions concerning whether, when or how foreign citizenship was acquired.

## Applicant significance

For applicants dealing with dual-nationality or foreign-passport questions, this is a foundational authority showing that the foreign-citizenship question is governed by the Citizenship Act and prescribed evidentiary rules.

## Limits of the case

It is an old constitutional decision. Current rules and amendments must still be checked for the precise procedure and evidence.

## Case 4.3 - Reena Chandy v. Union of India

Court: Kerala High Court Date: 7 September 2026

Citation / Case No.: WP(C) No. 28670 of 2024; 2026:KER:67947

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 4 and Section 9

## Facts

The petitioner claimed citizenship by descent based on an Indian father at the time of her birth in 1953. A foreign passport/citizenship issue arose because she had obtained Singapore citizenship while a minor.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether the applicant was a citizen by descent under Section 4.
- 2 Whether acquisition of foreign citizenship while a minor could be treated as voluntary acquisition for Section 9 purposes.
- 3 Whether the Central Government had applied the correct legal test to the status determination.

## Decision / holding

The High Court set aside the challenged determination and directed reconsideration, noting the finding that the petitioner was covered by Section 4 and that acquisition of Singapore citizenship while she was a minor could not simply be treated as her voluntary act.

## Applicant significance

This case is useful when the foreign-citizenship event occurred through parents while the claimant was a minor. The factual timeline and proof of how foreign citizenship was acquired become critical.

## Limits of the case

It is a High Court decision on the particular record and may be subject to further proceedings. Check current appellate status before relying on it.

## Part V - Registration, Residence and Naturalisation

## Case 5.1 - Shantanu Prakash v. Union of India

Court: Delhi High Court Date: 30 March 2026

Citation / Case No.: WP(C) 10428/2024

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 5(1)(a)

## Facts

The petition challenged rejection of a citizenship application under Section 5(1)(a). The stated reasons included adverse remarks based on FIRs reported by security and police agencies.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether the rejection decision was legally sustainable on the reasons recorded.
- 2 How the adverse police/security material affected the citizenship application.

## Decision / holding

The order records the challenge to the rejection and the statutory setting. The case is particularly relevant to the interaction between Section 5 applications and adverse police/security material.

## Applicant significance

Use this case when the core issue is a rejection based on criminal/security reports. The exact order and subsequent proceedings should be read before drawing a general rule.

## Limits of the case

The existence of FIRs does not by itself create a universal statutory bar in every citizenship category; the decision-maker's reasons, the applicable category and the procedural history matter.

## Case 5.2 - Munna Mohammed Grouse v. Union of India

Court: High Court of Karnataka Date: 22 April 2026

Citation / Case No.: 2026 High Court order

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 5(1)(f); Citizenship Rules

## Facts

The petition concerned an applicant relying on an earlier Indian-citizen parent and ordinary residence in India. The government argued that the Section 5(1)(f) requirements, the applicable form, undertaking and oath had to be satisfied.

## Legal issues

- 1 What statutory conditions applied to the registration claim.
- 2 Whether humanitarian/family considerations could replace statutory eligibility.

## Decision / holding

The order records the statutory eligibility framework and the government's position that the applicant had to satisfy the prescribed Section 5 route and Rules. It is useful primarily as an example of the court examining the statutory route rather than treating family hardship as an automatic substitute for eligibility.

## Applicant significance

Applicants should identify the exact Section 5 clause before relying on family relationships or humanitarian arguments.

## Limits of the case

Read the full order and subsequent history before treating it as a definitive precedent on the merits.

## Part VI - OCI

## Case 6.1 - Anushka Rengunthwar v. Union of India

Court: Supreme Court of India Date: 3 February 2023

Citation / Case No.: (2023) 11 SCC 209

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 7B; OCI notification dated 4 March 2021

## Facts

The case concerned OCI cardholders and admission/education-related privileges under the statutory OCI framework.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether OCI holders were entitled to parity with Indian citizens in the relevant educational-admission context.
- 2 How Section 7B and the governing notification defined the scope of OCI privileges.

## Decision / holding

The Supreme Court treated OCI as a statutory status distinct from citizenship and upheld the policy framework that did not grant OCI holders automatic parity with Indian citizens for the relevant admission issue.

## Applicant significance

OCI should be analyzed by the precise statutory right or notification involved. OCI status does not convert into citizenship and does not automatically create every right available to citizens.

## Limits of the case

The decision concerns the particular notification and subject matter. Check later OCI notifications for current entitlements.

## Case 6.2 - Khalid Jahangir Qazi through his Power of Attorney v. Union of India

Court: Delhi High Court Date: 11 August 2026 Citation / Case No.: LPA 1239/2024 with connected matters Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 7D; Foreigners Act

## Facts

The litigation concerned cancellation of OCI status and subsequent action under the foreigner-control framework. The court considered the distinct statutory position of OCI cardholders and the hearing safeguard in Section 7D.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether an OCI holder remains subject to foreigner law despite OCI privileges.
- 2 What procedural protection Section 7D provides before cancellation.
- 3 How OCI-specific safeguards interact with separate action under foreigner law.

## Decision / holding

The judgment emphasized that OCI cardholders are not Indian citizens and can remain subject to foreigner law. It also addressed the statutory requirement of a reasonable opportunity of hearing before cancellation under Section 7D and the relationship between OCI cancellation and foreigner-law action.

## Applicant significance

For an OCI holder facing cancellation or related immigration action, identify each statutory proceeding separately and check whether the applicable procedural safeguards were followed.

## Limits of the case

This is a High Court appellate decision and the litigation may continue. Check current appellate status and later orders.

## Part VII - Administrative Delay, Rejection and Remedies

## Case 7.1 - Gurmeet Singh Bains v. Union of India

Court: Punjab &amp; Haryana High Court Date: 30 August 2022

Citation / Case No.: CWP-13636-2022

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 5(1)(a); Article 226

## Facts

The petitioner, a Kenyan citizen, had a pending citizenship application. During the delay, the validity of his Kenyan visa/passport became an issue, and he sought directions concerning processing of his citizenship application and related immigration hardship.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether the High Court could intervene in the circumstances of a pending citizenship application.
- 2 How delay in citizenship processing interacted with the applicant's foreign passport/visa position.

## Decision / holding

The case illustrates the use of Article 226 in a citizenship-processing dispute and the practical consequences that delay can create for a foreign applicant.

## Applicant significance

For delayed applicants, preserve evidence of the pending application and explain the concrete legal/practical consequence of delay rather than merely alleging inconvenience.

## Limits of the case

The court's powers depend on the specific facts and statutory duties. A writ does not automatically compel grant of citizenship.

## Case 7.2 - Shantanu Prakash v. Union of India

Court: Delhi High Court Date: 30 March 2026

Citation / Case No.: WP(C) 10428/2024

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 5(1)(a); Article 226

## Facts

The applicant challenged a rejection of citizenship based on adverse police/security information.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether the administrative rejection was supported by the recorded reasons.
- 2 What role adverse security information played in the decision.

## Decision / holding

The order demonstrates a judicial review setting in which the legality of the citizenship authority's rejection is challenged rather than the court simply assuming the role of the original decision-maker.

## Applicant significance

Applicants challenging rejection should obtain the actual rejection order, identify the statutory basis, and attack the legally relevant defect rather than merely restating eligibility.

## Limits of the case

The cited order should be read in full, including any subsequent order or appeal.

## Part VIII - Deprivation and Citizenship-Status Disputes

## Case 8.1 - Nageshwar Mishra v. Union of India

Court: Allahabad High Court Date: 2 September 2020

Citation / Case No.: 2020 High Court decision

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 10; Constitution Articles 5-11

## Facts

The dispute concerned whether the statutory deprivation mechanism under Section 10 could be used against a person whose citizenship arose by birth.

## Legal issues

- 1 Who falls within the statutory class that can be deprived under Section 10.
- 2 Whether Section 10 could be used merely because a person was born in India.

## Decision / holding

The Court emphasized that Section 10 applies to the categories of citizens specified by the statute, including citizens by naturalisation and specified registration/constitutional categories. It rejected treating Section 10 as a general deprivation power over every Indian citizen.

## Applicant significance

For citizenship-status disputes, identify the original legal source of citizenship before analyzing Section 10.

## Limits of the case

The case is a High Court decision and should be read with the current statutory text and later authority.

## Primary source

[https://indiankanoon.org/doc/43163736/](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/43163736/)

## Case 8.2 - Ramesh Singh v. Smt. Sonia Gandhi

Court: Allahabad High Court Date: 11 July 2016

Citation / Case No.: 2016 High Court decision

Key provisions: Citizenship Act Sections 5, 9 and 10

## Facts

The litigation involved a challenge to the citizenship status of a person who had been registered as an Indian citizen. The court considered whether it could independently conduct a citizenship inquiry while the registration order remained operative.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether a court could simply disregard an existing citizenship registration order.
- 2 What statutory mechanisms existed for termination or deprivation.

## Decision / holding

The Court held that, so long as the registration order stood and had not been cancelled/withdrawn or otherwise displaced under the statutory mechanisms, the court could not simply treat the person as not being a citizen on its own.

## Applicant significance

Citizenship status should be attacked through the legally prescribed mechanism. An applicant or challenger should identify the operative certificate/order and the statutory route for disputing it.

## Limits of the case

The case concerns a specific election-related challenge and should not be generalized beyond the procedural point it addresses.

## Primary source

[https://indiankanoon.org/doc/149875120/](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/149875120/)

## Part IX - Section 6A / CAA Litigation

## Case 9.1 - In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, 1955

Court: Supreme Court of India Date: 17 October 2024 final decision Citation / Case No.: W.P.(C) No. 274/2009 and connected proceedings Key provisions: Citizenship Act Section 6A; Constitution

## Facts

The litigation challenged the constitutional validity and operation of Section 6A, the Assam Accord-related citizenship framework.

## Legal issues

- 1 Whether Section 6A was constitutionally valid.
- 2 How the statutory citizenship framework interacted with the Assam Accord and constitutional provisions.

## Decision / holding

The Supreme Court's 2024 judgment addressed the constitutional challenge to Section 6A and is a major authority for applicants whose facts fall within the Assam-specific framework.

## Applicant significance

Do not apply Section 6A as a generic citizenship route. It is a specialized statutory framework with date-specific migration categories.

## Limits of the case

The judgment is constitutionally significant but does not replace the need to examine the applicant's individual date, residence and documentary facts.

## 9.2 - Section 6B: current administrative litigation note

Section 6B implementation is more procedure-sensitive than many older citizenship categories because the 2024 Rules and subsequent 2026 changes created specific administrative arrangements. As of August 2026, MHA's current materials show a Collector-centered process for Section 6B applications in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Punjab, West Bengal, Assam except tribal areas, Tripura except tribal areas, and the Union Territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh. This is a current administrative rule/order rather than a court precedent, so it belongs in a case-law handbook as a cross-reference rather than being presented as a judicial holding.

## Source discipline

For current law, prioritize MHA/India Code/Gazette material. For case law, prioritize the Supreme Court or the relevant High Court's official judgment/order portal. Secondary repositories such as Indian Kanoon are useful for discovery and text access but should not replace verification against the original judgment when the case is legally important.
